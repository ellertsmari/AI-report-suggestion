The Sustainable Island 2027 · AI Use Policy, version 2

Use AI freely. Own every line. Show your work.

A proposal for the teachers' meeting on 2 October 2026. It builds on Heinz's draft from May. His principle, structure and most of his wording stay. v2 adds how students record their AI use, what they hand in, and how we grade it. It also suggests replacing the presentation on 21 January with group visits.

Summary

Three things every student needs to know

Allowed

Any AI, any part

  • Any tool, including an agent that builds features while the student directs and reviews it
  • One exception: design sprint interviews are with real people
Required

Notes, kept daily

  • A few daily bullet points: what the AI got wrong, what changed, what was learned
  • A daily account written by the AI itself from its session history
  • A team AI report built from both
Graded

Two rubric items

  • Understanding your code: each student walks a teacher through their own code
  • The AI report: each team's report and notes, after the project
  • We grade how critically students used AI, not how much

If you cannot explain a line of code to your teacher or a teammate, it does not belong in your project.The Golden Rule from Heinz's draft, unchanged in v2.

Where v2 starts

Heinz's draft set the foundation

In May, Heinz wrote the first shared AI policy for the Sustainable Island. Until then each school handled AI on its own. The draft settled the key question: students may use AI, and what we assess is whether they understand what they hand in. v2 would not exist without it.

January 2027

The policy during the project

  1. 11–15 Jan

    Online design sprint

    Teams interview real people. AI can help prepare the questions and sort the notes. Each student starts a notes file in the team repository.

  2. 18 Jan

    Meetup in Las Palmas

    Teams set up report/notes/ and agree who owns which part of the code.

  3. 19–28 Jan

    Project work

    Every day: students commit their notes and the AI's account. Teachers run code reviews and spot checks, which count toward Understanding your code.

  4. 21 Jan

    Group visits

    Pairs of teachers visit each group where it works and ask every group the same questions. Design, Design Sprint, UI and GitHub are graded. The teachers also look at the notes and give feedback (not graded). Option A: Understanding your code is graded here.

  5. 29 Jan

    Final presentation

    Product, Presentation and Q&A are graded. Teams submit the link to their AI report. Option B: Understanding your code is graded here.

  6. After

    Post assessment

    Teachers review the front end and back end code, and grade The AI report alongside it.

Daily, in the repository

The student's notes and the AI's own account

Heinz's log asks four good questions: what you asked, what came back, what you changed, what you learned. v2 asks them once a day instead of after every interaction, and adds an account the AI writes about itself. The two files check each other.

The prompt students use

Go through our sessions from today and the git log. Write a short, factual account: what you built or changed (with commit hashes), what I asked you to explain, where you were wrong and how it was caught, and what I changed in your code. Do not flatter me.
report/notes/student.mdExample
## Tuesday 19 January
- Asked for the recycling map. It put our API key in a VITE_ variable, so it was visible in the browser.
- Changed: moved the key to the server (e18a0b7), because anyone could copy it.
- Did not understand useEffect cleanup. Learned it from two explanations, then rewrote the fetch with AbortController.
- Decided on Postgres, not MongoDB as the AI suggested: our data is all related.
report/notes/student-ai.mdExample
## AI account, Tuesday 19 January
- Built the recycling map component (a41c9e2) and the /api/points CRUD routes (7d03b18).
- Explained useEffect cleanup twice. The student rewrote the fetch (c92f4d1).
- Wrong: I put the API key in a VITE_ variable, which exposes it in the browser. Caught by the student; moved to the server (e18a0b7).
- Changed by the student: I trusted the user id sent by the client. The server now reads it from the token.

Raw transcripts are not committed, because they can contain personal data and secret keys. Students keep them until grades are given, and a teacher may ask to see the relevant part during a code review. Tools without session history, such as a browser chat, get one line in the student's own notes.

21 January · suggestion

Group visits instead of presentations

On 21 January the groups do not present. Pairs of teachers visit each group where it works and ask every group the same questions. One teacher asks, and the other marks the answers on the rubric.

4 pairs

8 teachers work in pairs of two: one asks the questions, one marks.

~2 groups

per pair, with about 8 groups in the project.

30–35 min

per visit: about 15 minutes of group questions, plus 3–5 minutes per student if Understanding your code is graded here. In 2026 the groups had 3–4 students.

Why visits

  • No part of the classroom has to become a presentation area, which students complained about last year
  • Every group gets the same questions, so the marks are comparable
  • Students answer at their own screens, where they can show their work
  • Checking each student's code fits into the visit without an extra session

When do we judge each item?

Understanding your code: 21 or 29 January?

3–5 minutes per student. In 2026 there were 35 students in 9 groups of 3–4.

Option A · 21 January

During the group visits

For
  • The teachers are already at the group's screens, so it adds 12–20 minutes per visit and no extra session
  • Students get the result while there is still time to change how they work
  • The final presentation day stays as it is
Against
  • Only a few days of code exist
  • It judges the start of the project, not what was shipped
  • Students who own later features have little to show yet
Option B · 29 January

With the final presentation

For
  • All the code exists, so it checks what was actually shipped
  • It extends the Q&A the presentation already has
Against
  • It adds 3–5 minutes per student to a day that is already full: about 2–3 hours for 35 students if done one after another
  • No time to improve afterwards
  • Students are tired and nervous on the last day

The AI report: after the project

Suggestion

Grade The AI report in the post assessment, together with the thorough review of the front end and back end code.

  • The report is only finished at the end of the project
  • Teachers review the code at that point anyway, and the report explains that code
  • The git history of the notes is complete, so we can see whether they were kept day by day
  • It takes no time from the weeks in Las Palmas

Assignment/AI-policy.md

The full v2 text

Show the policy as students will read it

1. Purpose

This document defines how AI tools may be used in The Sustainable Island 2027 project. Students are encouraged to use AI — but must maintain genuine understanding of every line of code they submit.

AI is a learning tool, not a replacement for understanding.

The policy is the same for every student in the project, whatever school you come from. Your own school's exam rules still apply on top of it. If you are unsure, ask your teacher.

2. Permitted AI Use

Students may use AI tools (ChatGPT, GitHub Copilot, Claude, etc.) in every part of the project. For example, to:

  • Generate boilerplate code or starter templates
  • Understand concepts, syntax, or error messages
  • Explore approaches to a technical problem
  • Review and improve code they have already written
  • Help write documentation and comments
  • Build features with an AI agent that you direct, run and review
  • Generate reports, explanations of complex topics, and visual comparisons of different solutions

One exception: the interviews in the design sprint. The problem your team solves must come from talking to real people. AI may help you prepare questions and sort your notes, but it must never invent interviews, answers or users.

3. The Core Requirement

It does not matter whether code was written by the student, a teammate, or an AI. Every student must be able to explain any code within their area of contribution.

The Golden Rule

If you cannot explain a line of code to your teacher or a teammate, it does not belong in your project.

Understanding will be checked through code reviews, a walkthrough with a teacher, and unannounced spot-checks.

4. What Is Required When Using AI

4.1 AI Notes (every project day)

Each student keeps a notes file in the team repository: report/notes/<your-name>.md. Add to it and commit it at the end of every project day. A few bullet points per day is enough:

  • What you asked the AI to do, and what it got wrong
  • What you changed and why
  • What you did not understand, and what you learned
  • Decisions you made (including suggestions from the AI that you did not follow)

These notes become your team's AI report (section 5).

4.2 The AI's Own Account (every project day)

Most AI agents keep a history of your sessions. At the end of each project day, ask your agent to write an account of how it was used, and commit it as report/notes/<your-name>-ai.md. You can use this prompt:

Go through our sessions from today and the git log. Write a short, factual account: what you built or changed (with commit hashes), what I asked you to explain, where you were wrong and how it was caught, and what I changed in your code. Do not flatter me.

If you used a tool without history (for example a chat in the browser or on your phone), add one line about it to your own notes.

Do not commit raw chat transcripts. They can contain personal data or secret keys. Keep them until grades are given; a teacher may ask to see the relevant part during a code review.

4.3 Commit Ownership

You must be able to explain every commit you have made. Read, understand, and if needed modify AI-generated code before you commit it. If your tool adds a line such as Co-Authored-By to a commit, leave it there.

4.4 Team Review Before Merge (recommended)

Before merging AI-generated code, explain it to at least one teammate. If you cannot explain it, it is not ready to merge. This is a habit we recommend. It is not graded.

5. The AI Report

Each team publishes an AI report as a web page, report/index.html in the repository, and submits its link together with the other project links. Use your AI to build it from your notes: turning your work into a clear, visual report is part of the exercise. The report has these sections:

  1. Try it – link to the live app (and a QR code)
  2. The problem – what you learned from the interviews in the design sprint
  3. Decision log – the main choices (stack, database, authentication, hosting…): what the AI suggested, what you chose, and why. Compare the solutions you considered in a visual way.
  4. What the AI got wrong – the mistakes you found and how you fixed them, with links to the commits. This is the most valuable part of the report.
  5. Tutorials – one short, interactive explanation per student of a complex part of the code that student owns
  6. How we used AI – which tools, what for, based on everyone's notes and the AI's own accounts

Thorough beats pretty. Evidence beats prose.

We grade how critically you used AI, not how much you used it. We do not use AI detectors: they are unreliable, and they wrongly flag people who write in a second language, which is all of us.

7. If You Cannot Explain Your Code

Using AI that you have declared in your notes is never penalised. What matters is whether you understand the result.

If you cannot explain your code, the following may apply:

  • The section may be marked incomplete
  • You may be asked to rewrite it independently
  • A one-on-one technical interview may be requested

This is not a punishment — it is a way to make sure the project is a real learning experience.

8. A Note on Integrity

Using AI is fine. Submitting AI output you do not understand — and presenting it as your own — is not.

AI can make you a better developer, but only if you engage with it critically and take responsibility for what ends up in your code.

Before you commit, ask yourself:

  • ✓ Can I explain every line?
  • ✓ Did I note what the AI got wrong today?
  • ✓ Are my notes and the AI's account for today committed?
  • ✓ Would I be comfortable walking a teacher through this right now?
